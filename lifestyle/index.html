LearnWhy the plan says what it says

Short lessons. Each takes a minute. Open the ones that match your plan.

Diet does the losing, exercise does the keeping
  • Exercise alone usually takes off only 2–4 kg. The body compensates by moving less the rest of the day and sometimes eating a little more.
  • Every extra 30 minutes of weekly cardio adds about half a kilo of loss over a programme. Useful, but small next to food.
  • Diet plus exercise beats either alone by the 12-month mark, because exercise is what stops the weight coming back.
  • People who keep weight off for years do about an hour of activity a day, mostly walking.
  • So: eat for the loss, move for the maintenance, lift for the muscle.
Protein: the one number most people get wrong
  • During weight loss you need 1.2–1.6 g per kg of body weight a day, about double the usual Indian intake.
  • A typical Gujarati day (thepla, chai, rotli, rice, a little dal) gives 40–50 g. A 75 kg person needs 90–120 g.
  • Spread it: 25–30 g at each main meal. One big protein dinner does not work as well.
  • Vegetarian sources that add up: dal and chana (7–9 g a katori), paneer (9 g per 50 g), curd and hung curd (5–10 g), soya chunks (13 g per 25 g dry), tofu, besan, peanuts, milk.
  • Protein keeps you full, protects muscle, and burns a little energy just being digested.
  • Healthy kidneys handle 1.6 g/kg fine. If your eGFR is under 60, the limit is 0.8 g/kg and a doctor sets it.
Indian bodies, Indian cut-offs
  • Indians develop diabetes and heart disease at lower weights than Europeans, mostly because fat sits around the belly and organs.
  • So the cut-offs are lower: BMI 23 and above is overweight, 25 and above is obesity.
  • Waist matters more than weight: 80 cm or more for women, 90 cm or more for men, is abdominal obesity even at a normal BMI.
  • Easy check: waist divided by height should be under 0.5.
  • Losing 5–10% of body weight improves blood sugar, blood pressure and liver fat. You do not have to reach the "ideal" weight to get most of the benefit.
Cardio: it is about joints and sticking with it
  • Each kilo of body weight puts about 2–3 kilos through the knee when walking, 3–4 when jogging or going downstairs, and about 1 kilo on a stationary bike.
  • Each kilo you lose takes about 4 kilos of load off the knee with every step. Joints get safer as you go.
  • Novice runners with a BMI over 25 get injured 25% of the time; over 30, much more. Start with walking, cycling or water, and earn running later.
  • HIIT does not burn more fat than steady cardio when the calories are equal. The famous "28% more" study was retracted. Most people also quit HIIT within months.
  • Aim for 150 minutes a week to start, 250–300 to lose well and keep it off. Ten-minute walks after meals count.
  • The best cardio is the one you will still be doing in six months.
Strength training: muscle is the asset
  • When you lose weight without lifting, a quarter to a third of what goes is muscle. In the semaglutide trial, 38% of weight lost was lean tissue.
  • Less muscle means a slower metabolism, weaker bones, more falls after 60, and faster regain.
  • Two sessions a week of 6–8 movements (squat, hinge, push, pull, step, core) is enough to keep muscle. Bodyweight and bands work.
  • Progress by small steps: more reps, then more weight, then more sets. Pain above 3/10 means step back.
  • After 60, add balance work three days a week and stand up fast, sit down slow. That is what prevents falls.
  • Women do not get bulky from this. They get stronger bones and a smaller waist.
GLP-1 drugs: what they do and what they do not
  • Semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide and tirzepatide copy gut hormones that slow the stomach and quieten appetite. Intake falls by 16–39%.
  • Trials show 15–20% weight loss over about 16 months. That is far more than diet alone.
  • Common side effects: nausea (up to 4 in 10), constipation, diarrhoea, tiredness, dizziness, hair thinning. Small low-fat meals, protein first, and 2–2.5 litres of fluid help.
  • Because you eat less, you can run short of protein, iron, B12, calcium and vitamin D. Protein first, and ask about a multivitamin.
  • Rare but serious: severe belly pain, vomiting over a day, low sugar if also on insulin or a sulfonylurea, sudden painless vision loss. See a doctor.
  • When the drug stops, about two-thirds of the lost weight returns within a year unless habits and muscle are in place. The drug does the first year; you do the rest.
  • Not for pregnancy or breastfeeding. Stop semaglutide at least 2 months before trying to conceive.
Diabetes: walk after you eat
  • A 10–15 minute walk after each meal lowers the after-meal glucose spike more than one long walk at another time.
  • Never go more than two days without activity; the effect of each session on insulin sensitivity fades in 48 hours.
  • Losing 5–7% of body weight with 150 minutes of weekly activity cuts the risk of prediabetes turning into diabetes by about half.
  • On insulin or a sulfonylurea: check glucose before exercise, carry something sweet, and remember that lows can come hours later.
  • Feet: closed shoes, soft indoor footwear instead of bare feet, daily look at the soles.
Salt and blood pressure
  • Indians eat 8–11 g of salt a day. The target is under 5 g, about one level teaspoon in total, including what is in food.
  • Most of it hides in papad, pickle, farsan, namkeen, pickled chutneys, bread, biscuits and restaurant food, not in the salt shaker.
  • Cutting salt to target lowers blood pressure about as much as one medicine.
  • Potassium from food (banana, coconut water, dal, spinach, curd) helps, unless you have kidney disease.
  • Exercise, less alcohol and a 5–10% weight loss each lower blood pressure further.
The Gujarati plate: five fixes
  • Take the sugar and jaggery out of dal, kadhi and sabzi. Use kokum, lemon or tomato for the sour note.
  • Fried farsan is the biggest hidden calorie source. Swap fafda, gathiya, sev and bhajia for khaman, dhokla, roasted chana or sprouts.
  • Rotli count down, protein count up: two plain rotli with extra sabzi, dal and curd beats four with ghee.
  • Chai: four sweet cups a day is 120 calories of sugar. Half a teaspoon or none.
  • Half the plate vegetables, a quarter protein, a quarter bajra, jowar or whole wheat. Rice one katori, not two.
Fasting, festivals and Garba
  • Farali food can be heavy: sabudana, fried potato, sugar. Rajgira or sama with curd or paneer, peanuts and fruit is the lighter version.
  • Water-only fasts are unsafe on insulin, sulfonylureas, SGLT2 drugs or GLP-1 drugs, in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and after 75.
  • Garba is real exercise, moderate to vigorous. Eat a protein snack before, drink water, and skip the late fried meal after.
  • Festival weeks are maintenance weeks: one plate, one sweet, a walk after. Do not try to lose weight during Diwali; aim not to gain.
  • Ramadan with diabetes needs a medicine review before the month starts.
Sleep, stress and steps
  • Under 6 hours of sleep raises hunger hormones and predicts regain. Seven to nine hours is part of the plan.
  • Stress eating is real. Yoga, pranayama and Yoga Nidra lower it; so does a walk.
  • Steps: add 1,000 a day every two weeks up to 7,000–10,000. After 60, the benefit flattens around 6,000–8,000.
  • Attach new habits to old ones: walk after lunch, protein with morning tea. A habit takes about two months to become automatic.
  • Weigh weekly, same day and time. Daily weight bounces 1–2 kg with water and salt; weekly trend is what counts.
Plateaus and regain
  • As you lose weight you burn fewer calories, so the same food that produced loss now produces maintenance. Plateaus are normal, not failure.
  • After every 8–10% lost, hold the new weight for 4–8 weeks before cutting again. It settles the body and the habits.
  • The 2 kg rule: when 2 kg returns, run a two-week reset of food logging and 30 extra minutes a week.
  • Realistic pace: 5 kg in about 10 weeks, 10 kg in 5 months, 20 kg in 9–12 months, 30 kg or more in 1–2 years and often with medical help.
  • Half a kilo a week kept off beats two kilos a week regained.
Pregnancy, after birth, menopause and after 60
  • Pregnancy is not for losing weight. Gain targets depend on pre-pregnancy BMI (5–9 kg if obese, 11.5–16 kg if normal). Walking, swimming, prenatal yoga and moderate strength work are safe; stop for bleeding, pain, dizziness or breathlessness.
  • After birth: pelvic floor from day one, walking from week one, strength from week six, running not before three months. Caesarean: no heavy lifting or impact for 12 weeks.
  • Menopause: muscle and bone go faster. Protein 1.2 g/kg, calcium, vitamin D, strength and balance training. Keep the deficit gentle.
  • After 60: a small deficit only, protein 1.2–1.5 g/kg, strength twice a week, balance three times. After 75, losing weight is only worth it if BMI is over 27 and the doctor agrees.
Myths that cost people months
  • "Crunches burn belly fat." No. Fat comes off the whole body; the belly is last because it is where Indians store the most.
  • "Sweating means burning fat." Sweat is water. It comes back with the next glass.
  • "Skipping breakfast saves calories." Most people eat it back by evening. Breakfast with protein helps control the day.
  • "Detox juices and teas." Your liver and kidneys detox. Juice is sugar without the fibre.
  • "Protein harms the kidneys." Not in healthy kidneys. The limit applies only when eGFR is under 60.
  • "Yoga alone will do it." Yoga helps sleep, stress, back pain and blood pressure. For weight it is an add-on.
  • "Lifting makes women bulky." It makes them stronger, with better bones and a smaller waist.
  • "Ghee is healthy, so more is fine." A teaspoon is fine. It is still 45 calories a teaspoon.
  • "Brown sugar, jaggery and honey are better than sugar." Same calories, same effect on glucose.
  • "HIIT burns fat for hours after." The afterburn is small, under 100 calories. Steady cardio for longer wins on total.
